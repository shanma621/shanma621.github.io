Campaign Spending and Electoral Success

An analysis of U.S. House general elections from 2010 through 2024 examining how candidate and independent spending relate to vote share, winning, and possible diminishing returns.

Read the Case Study View Dashboard View Code

Overview

This project investigated the relationship between campaign spending and electoral success in U.S. House general elections from 2010 through 2024, including whether additional spending became less effective at higher levels.

I combined Federal Election Commission candidate-finance data, candidate-specific independent expenditures, and MIT Election Data and Science Lab election results across eight election cycles. The analysis measured statistical associations rather than claiming that spending caused electoral outcomes.

What I Did

  • Collected independent-expenditure records through the OpenFEC API
  • Integrated campaign-finance and election-result data from multiple sources
  • Standardized candidate names and validated candidate-election matches
  • Built and compared correlation, OLS, logistic, and quadratic models
  • Created analytical visuals and an interactive Tableau dashboard

Tools & Skills

  • Python 3.12, pandas, NumPy, Requests, SciPy, and statsmodels
  • Jupyter Notebooks and Visual Studio Code
  • API collection, pagination, retries, and error handling
  • Data integration, entity matching, and validation
  • Regression analysis, model comparison, and Tableau

Data Preparation

Building the Analytical Dataset

  • Filtered all sources to U.S. House general elections from 2010 through 2024
  • Preserved raw files separately from cleaned and processed data
  • Standardized names, election years, states, districts, and election types
  • Handled fusion-ticket, write-in, missing, and malformed records
  • Separated independent expenditures into candidate support and opposition totals
  • Aggregated each source to a consistent candidate-election level before joining

The Hard Part: Candidate Matching

The FEC and MIT datasets did not share a candidate identifier. I matched records using standardized candidate names, election year, state, and district, then validated the results. Ambiguous and unmatched records remained separate instead of being forced into the analytical dataset.

That choice reduced coverage slightly, but protected the analysis from incorrect spending assignments.

Analysis

Analytical Process

  • Used descriptive statistics and visualizations to evaluate distributions and outliers
  • Applied log transformations because campaign spending was highly right-skewed
  • Measured relationships with Pearson correlation and OLS regression
  • Used logistic regression to examine the probability of winning
  • Calculated each candidate's share of total spending within the race
  • Tested diminishing returns with spending deciles, quadratic regression, and an F-test

Controlled Model

The final multivariable model evaluated candidate spending while accounting for independent expenditures, incumbency, party, and election year. Residual plots and model comparisons were used to assess fit and remaining error.

The models were used to describe broad relationships, not to make precise predictions or causal claims.

Key Results

Spending and Vote Share

  • Log-transformed candidate spending had a strong positive correlation with vote share (r = .793)
  • The controlled model explained 72.8% of the variation in vote share
  • Candidate spending remained statistically significant after other electoral factors were included

Relative Spending

  • Candidate share of race spending had an even stronger correlation with vote share (r = .883)
  • Each 10-percentage-point increase in spending share was associated with about 2.78 times greater odds of winning
  • Relative financial position provided more context than total dollars alone

Independent Expenditures

Independent spending supporting a candidate was positively associated with vote share, while spending opposing a candidate was negatively associated with vote share in the controlled model.

Diminishing Returns

A simpler model initially suggested diminishing returns, but the quadratic term was no longer significant after other electoral factors were included. The analysis did not identify one reliable spending level where additional spending consistently became less effective.

Limitations

  • The analysis used observational data, so association should not be interpreted as causation
  • Some candidate records could not be matched reliably and were excluded from the primary dataset
  • Unmeasured candidate and race characteristics may also influence both spending and electoral performance
  • Complete race-level spending comparisons required sufficient coverage for all major candidates in a race
